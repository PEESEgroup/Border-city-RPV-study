# Manuscript Blueprint

## Working title
Economic returns and policy frictions, not wealth, shape rooftop solar divergence in neighboring border cities

## One-sentence take-home message
Rooftop solar disparities across neighboring border cities follow recurring patterns shaped more by economic returns and policy frictions than by wealth alone, with distinct mechanisms across building types.

## Core research question
Why do neighboring border cities, despite identical solar resource, geographic proximity and often strong economic linkages, diverge in rooftop PV adoption, and to what extent are these differences shaped by wealth, economic returns, and policy frictions?

## Central argument
Neighboring border cities do not converge naturally in rooftop PV adoption; instead, they diverge through recurring patterns that reflect the joint effects of project-level economic returns and policy frictions. Wealth is an incomplete explanation, because city pairs can exhibit system-wide leadership, sectoral divergence, or even income reversal depending on how return conditions and regulatory frictions align across rooftop segments.


## Main contributions
1. We build a cross-border urban rooftop PV framework that links building-level PV mapping with city-level economic and policy indicators across 12 cities in 6 border-city pairs.
2. We reveal three recurring patterns of rooftop PV divergence across borders—system-wide leadership, sectoral divergence, and income reversal—showing that cross-border solar divides are systematic rather than idiosyncratic.
3. We demonstrate that these cross-border disparities are driven more by realized economic returns and policy frictions than by wealth differences alone, offering a new explanation for uneven urban solar uptake.
4. We show that the drivers of rooftop PV deployment differ across building types, with large-roof non-residential deployment more closely associated with economic returns and administrative frictions, and residential deployment more sensitive to revenue-side policy design.

## Narrative logic
- First, we map building-level rooftop PV across six border-city pairs and show that neighboring cities diverge in three recurring ways rather than converging.
- Then, we demonstrate that overall cross-border PV leadership is better aligned with economic returns and policy frictions than with wealth differences alone.
- Next, we unpack sectoral heterogeneity and show that residential and non-residential rooftop PV respond to different policy and economic mechanisms.
- Finally, we discuss what these findings imply for urban solar transitions, border-city governance, and segment-specific policy design.

### Discussions
Goal:
Interpret the three-pattern framework and position the study within urban energy transitions, comparative city governance, and policy design for border-city decarbonization.

Key points:
- Border-city rooftop PV divergence is shaped more by policy-adjustable return conditions and deployment frictions than by wealth differences alone.
- Geographic proximity does not guarantee convergence in urban solar adoption; neighboring cities can follow sharply different solar trajectories under different market and institutional regimes.
- Wealth is an incomplete proxy for urban solar readiness.
- Residential and non-residential rooftop PV follow distinct economic and policy logics and should not be treated as a single deployment category.
- Although this study focuses on six border-city pairs, the framework is intended to speak more broadly to global border cities where adjacent urban areas share similar geographic conditions but operate under different regulatory, pricing, and administrative systems.

Main interpretation:
The central insight is that rooftop PV divergence across neighboring border cities is generated by the alignment—or misalignment—of realized economic returns, policy frictions, and rooftop opportunity structures, rather than by wealth differences alone. The three-pattern framework shows that border-city solar divides are systematic and policy-shaped, not merely case-specific outcomes. More broadly, border cities provide a powerful lens for understanding how institutions and markets unevenly shape urban decarbonization across otherwise comparable urban environments.

Policy implications:
1. **Wealth is not destiny: policy-adjustable friction and return conditions shape the solar gap.**  
   Cross-border PV disparities should not be interpreted as a simple consequence of macroeconomic wealth differences. Cases such as **El Paso–Juárez** suggest that stronger export conditions, lower PV capital costs, and more favorable project economics can offset income differences and produce unexpectedly strong PV performance on the lower-income side. More generally, the key policy levers for narrowing urban solar gaps are those that governments can adjust directly: export compensation, electricity pricing, capital costs, financing access, and administrative efficiency.

2. **System-wide PV leadership emerges where favorable returns are reinforced by supportive policy environments.**  
   The leading cases in this study show that system-wide advantage does not arise from a single instrument, but from the combined effect of stronger returns and more enabling institutions. Higher electricity prices, stronger export compensation, and higher blended solar value can improve the economic attractiveness of rooftop PV, while streamlined permitting, interconnection, and implementation rules can translate these returns into actual deployment. This logic extends beyond the six city pairs studied here and may apply to many border cities worldwide. For cities such as **El Paso**, a key policy implication is to improve export compensation so that exported solar electricity more closely matches the value of avoided retail consumption. For cities such as **Hong Kong** and **Monaco**, where returns can already be attractive, the main barrier may lie less in project economics than in institutional and procedural inefficiency, especially for **non-residential rooftop PV**.

3. **For lower-adoption border cities, large-roof non-residential PV is a strategic frontier for scaling urban solar.**  
   Commercial, industrial, logistics, and other large-roof segments can offer a high-impact pathway for expanding urban PV, particularly where household adoption remains constrained. Cases such as **Shenzhen** and **Nice** suggest that deployment can be accelerated when administrative frictions are reduced and project returns are strengthened for large-roof systems. Compared with residential PV, non-residential rooftop deployment is often more sensitive to administrative barriers, interconnection procedures, ownership arrangements, and project economics. This implies that many lower-adoption border cities—not only those examined here—could narrow their solar gaps by prioritizing large-roof PV through targeted permitting reform, better compensation design, industrial-roof programs, and lower transaction costs.

Policy implications for broader border-city governance:
- Replace one-size-fits-all rooftop PV policy with segment-specific design.
- Improve revenue-side rules for residential PV through stronger export compensation, simpler settlement, and greater policy certainty.
- Improve non-residential PV through better project economics, lower administrative friction, and targeted support for large-roof deployment.
- Treat policy-adjustable return and friction conditions, rather than city wealth alone, as the primary levers for narrowing rooftop PV gaps across border cities globally.

Limitations:
- Small sample of six border-city pairs.
- Simplified policy-friction scoring framework and possible subjectivity in coding.
- Economic modeling is based on standardized assumptions rather than project-specific load profiles and financing conditions.
- Building classification and PV mapping uncertainty may propagate into utilization metrics.
- The cross-sectional design does not fully identify causality or policy timing effects.
- The broader applicability of the framework to other border-city pairs should be validated with additional cities and longitudinal policy data.
